Special Education Innovation Initiative

Remove barriers. Measure results.

RBBCSC already provides special-education services and may already use many assistive technologies with individual students. The question is not whether technology exists. The question is whether we have a consistent system for identifying barriers, evaluating options, training the people supporting students and measuring whether the intervention actually improves communication, learning, mobility, participation or independence.

“The question shouldn't simply be, ‘What can't this student do?’ We should also ask, ‘What barrier is preventing them from doing it—and can we remove it?’”

This is not a shopping list. Innovation does not mean buying the newest device. It means finding the right support for the individual student and proving that it helps.

Under IDEA, assistive technology must be considered when an IEP is developed, reviewed or revised. Federal guidance also emphasizes that AT services can involve evaluation, selection, acquisition, customization, training, technical assistance and implementation support—not merely handing a student a device.

What should we evaluate?

Examples worth studying

Communication

AAC, dynamic speech-generating devices, eye-gaze AAC, switch-access communication, low-tech and tactile communication, and emerging AI-assisted AAC where appropriate.

Physical & Computer Access

Eye-gaze computer control, head tracking, switch scanning, alternative keyboards and mice, adaptive mounting, seating and positioning.

Vision & Neurological Access

CVI-informed classroom design, reduced visual complexity, adapted displays, tactile alternatives and individualized screen/interface design.

Mobility & Independence

Supported or power mobility, environmental controls, adaptive switches, accessible robotics, smart-device controls and accessible vocational technology.

Instruction & Data

Digital progress monitoring, communication-frequency tracking, participation and independence measures, AAC outcomes and IEP-linked intervention data.

Training & Implementation

Clear responsibilities for teachers, special educators, paraprofessionals, SLPs, OT/PT, AT specialists or coaches and families. Buying AAC is not the same as successfully implementing AAC.

Public schools are already building systems around AT and AAC.

Other public-school models

Programs around the country use combinations of device loan libraries, switch access, eye gaze, alternative computer access, adapted equipment and professional development. The goal is to study systems—not copy a large district dollar-for-dollar.

A realistic RBBCSC concept

Assistive Technology & Innovation Program

Consistent evaluation

Develop a repeatable way to identify barriers involving communication, vision, mobility, sensory access, physical access and curriculum access.

Trial before major purchases

Explore a centralized trial library—or partnerships that provide access—to AAC devices, eye-gaze equipment, switches, mounts and adaptive input tools when trials are appropriate.

Implementation ownership

Make clear who is responsible for helping the student use the system across the day and across environments.

Training

Technology cannot help if the adults supporting the student do not know how to use, model, maintain or troubleshoot it.

Measure outcomes

Establish appropriate baselines and measure communication attempts, successful communication, participation, peer interaction, academic access, movement, independence and IEP progress.

Scale what works

Expand approaches that show measurable benefit. Modify or discontinue approaches that do not.

A unique local opportunity

Connect STEAM + Engineering + Special Education

RBBCSC already has meaningful STEAM and design infrastructure. We should explore whether those capabilities can support an Adaptive Design Program under appropriate professional supervision.

Existing capabilities to explore

3D printing, CAD/design, electronics, microcontrollers, fabrication and engineering.

Possible adaptive projects

Device mounts, switch mounts, grips, classroom access tools, communication-device supports and individualized learning materials.

This is an idea to investigate—not a promise of cost savings or a substitute for qualified specialists. It could potentially create authentic student design challenges while solving real accessibility problems.

Listen first

Questions RBBCSC should answer before deciding what needs to change

  1. How many students currently use AAC, and how many use high-tech speech-generating devices?
  2. What eye-gaze equipment and expertise are currently available?
  3. How many students use switch access?
  4. Who performs assistive-technology evaluations?
  5. Does RBBCSC maintain AT/AAC trial equipment, or use outside trial/loan programs?
  6. Can students trial devices before significant purchases when appropriate?
  7. What AT/AAC training is provided to teachers and paraprofessionals?
  8. How does the district measure whether AT improves student outcomes?
  9. What CVI-specific expertise is available?
  10. How much is currently spent on assistive technology, and what outside specialists or organizations are used?

These are information-gathering questions, not accusations. Publicly available information reviewed to date does not tell us everything RBBCSC may already provide through individual IEPs.
