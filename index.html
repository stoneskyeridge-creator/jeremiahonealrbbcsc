Parent & Special Education Resources

What is a SEPAC?

A Special Education Parent Advisory Council is a group of parents, caregivers, educators and community members who work together to improve special education across a school district.

It gives families a consistent way to raise district-wide concerns, identify needs, share ideas and collaborate with school leadership.

It is not just for families with a current complaint. A SEPAC can help improve communication, services, staff training, inclusion and outcomes for students receiving special education services throughout RBBCSC.

🏫 Leadership Connected to the Classroom

Board and district leadership should make a deliberate effort to understand what is happening across all grade levels and programs—general education, special education, career programs and support operations. Being hands-on does not mean interfering with educators; it means listening to the people doing the work and seeing classroom realities before making decisions that affect them.

Ideas Worth Researching

The school corporation itself can be a learning environment—not just its classrooms.

RBBCSC already relies on professionals and contractors doing dozens of different kinds of work. Why not explore appropriate ways for students to learn from the people already doing it? Depending on the role, that might include job shadowing, internships, work-based learning, apprenticeships, classroom demonstrations, mentoring or supervised student projects.

A student interested in business might learn how budgets, purchasing, spreadsheets, accounting and documentation work. A future mechanic could learn about transportation and bus maintenance. A student interested in engineering or the skilled trades could see how school buildings, HVAC, electrical systems, construction and facilities operations actually work. A student interested in technology might contribute to an appropriate supervised project involving webpages, programming, lab environments, networking or internal tools.

When outside contractors perform major work for RBBCSC, we should also explore whether appropriate student learning opportunities can be part of those relationships. RBBCSC does not have to build every career program itself—employers, unions, colleges, apprenticeship programs and training partners may help expand what students can experience.

Safety, privacy, cybersecurity, student age, labor agreements and other requirements obviously matter. Some ideas will work. Some won't. Others may lead us to something better. The responsible approach is to research the possibilities, talk to students and educators, work with professionals and partners, understand the costs and requirements, and determine what actually makes sense for RBBCSC.

Look at every department. Look at every major contract. Look at the professionals already working in and around our schools. Then ask one more question: Is there an appropriate opportunity here for a student to learn?

I don't have all the answers. I do promise to keep asking the questions.
